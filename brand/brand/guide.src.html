01 — The Name

Storystick

A story stick is the oldest planning tool there is: a length of scrap marked with every critical dimension of a job. Instead of re-reading a tape forty times and making forty chances to be wrong, you mark it once and carry the stick.

Storystick is that idea as software. Capture a dimension once, and it carries through every drawing, every elevation, every cut list — unchanged, unrounded, unmistaken.

The name also carries a second reading that matters. In building, a story is a level of a structure. And in the ordinary sense, every project has one: what it was, what it's becoming, what it took.

The three readings

story stickThe marked layout stick. Precision carried, not remembered.
storeyA level of a building. The product spans furniture to floors.
storyWhat you're making, and why. The human half.

Written as one word, title case: Storystick. Never "Story Stick," "StoryStick," or "story-stick."

02 — Positioning

What Storystick is

Storystick is a design and drafting tool for people who build things. Precise enough for a permit set, simple enough for a Saturday project.

The gap it fills

Professional CAD is overbuilt and overpriced for a homeowner. Consumer room planners are toys the moment you need a real dimension. Storystick sits in the middle and scales in both directions.

The core promise

One model, many outputs. Draw it once and get the plan, the elevation, the section, the materials list, and the cut list — all in agreement, all the time.

What it is not

Not a rendering showroom. Not a BIM replacement. Not a project-management suite. Storystick draws and dimensions; it does not try to run your business.

Brand personality

Precise

Every number is real. Nothing is "about."

Plainspoken

Shop language over software language.

Unintimidating

Expertise is welcome. It is never required.

Made-by-hand

Digital tool, physical outcome. Never forget the sawdust.

04 — Logo Suite

The mark

{{svg:mark-nobg}}

The mark is a story pole standing on a floor line. Three notches are cut into the pole — the marks you carry. One of them is transferred out to the right in cedar: the moment a measurement leaves the stick and becomes something you build to.

The notches are knocked out, not painted. The mark is a single-color shape plus one accent line, so it sits correctly on any background without a second version.

Geometry is built on a 120 × 120 grid with an 8-unit base module. Corner radii, stroke weights, and the notch depth are all multiples of it.

Define X as the width of the pole in the mark (34 units on the 120-unit grid — roughly 28% of the mark's height). Keep at least X of empty space on every side of any lockup. Nothing crosses it: no type, no rules, no photo edges, no other logos.

07 — Color

Blueprint and cedar

Two halves of one idea. The blues come from drafting — cyanotype paper, chalk lines, the cool light of a screen at 11pm. Cedar comes from the material side: sawdust, warm wood, a pencil behind the ear. Everything else is structure.

08 — Accessibility

Contrast rules

Measured against WCAG 2.1. Normal text needs 4.5:1; large text (18.66 px bold or 24 px regular) and UI boundaries need 3:1.

{{CONTRAST}}
ForegroundBackgroundRatioNormal textLarge / UI

Cedar is not a text color on light

Cedar #D98235 hits only 2.66:1 on Vellum. Use it for shapes, fills, lines, and icons — never for body copy on a light background. When cedar must carry text on light, use Cedar Deep #9E5813 (4.95:1).

On dark, cedar is fine

Cedar on Blueprint reaches 5.46:1 and passes for normal text. This is why the dark canvas is the natural home for cedar highlights — selection, active dimension, snap callouts.

Color is never the only signal. Snap states, errors, and validity always pair color with a shape, icon, or label change — a meaningful share of the woodworking and construction audience has some form of color vision deficiency, and job-site screens are read in direct sun.

12 — Voice & Tone

How Storystick talks

Like a good shop teacher: knows more than you, never makes you feel it, tells you the number.

Use the trade's words

Kerf, rip, dado, stud bay, on-center, header, rise and run. Not "parametric assembly," "entity," or "workspace object." If a carpenter wouldn't say it on site, don't put it in the UI.

State the number

Never "approximately" when the value is known. "33-1/2 in." beats "about 33 and a half inches." Vagueness is the one thing this tool exists to eliminate.

Second person, active, present

"Draw a wall, then set its height." Not "Walls may be created and subsequently modified."

Errors name the fix

"This wall is 1/4 in. short of the corner — snap it?" Not "Invalid geometry." Every error message ends with something the user can press.

Say this, not that

Say

Draw it before you build it.

Your cut list updates as you draw.

That stud bay is 14-1/2 in. Want to snap it to 16 on-center?

Works in feet-and-inches or millimeters. Switch anytime.

Start with a wall.

Not

Unleash your design potential.

Automatically generate a bill of materials.

Invalid geometry detected.

Full support for multiple unit systems and conversion workflows.

Get started by creating your first project workspace.

Things we never say

  • "Easy." Building is not easy. Drawing it can be clearer, faster, and more certain — say that instead.
  • "Just." As in "just drag the wall." It quietly blames the user when it doesn't work.
  • "Pro" as a slur or a gate. The hobbyist is not a lesser user, and the architect is not a different species.
  • Anything implying the drawing is a substitute for a licensed professional where one is legally required. Storystick produces drawings; it does not stamp them.

Before you launch

  • Run a US federal trademark search (USPTO TESS) in Class 9 and Class 42 for "Storystick" and near neighbors before you spend on print or file an application. This guide's name screening covered live products and domains, not registry records.
  • Secure storystick.app, storystick.com, and the matching handles on the channels your audience actually uses — YouTube and Instagram carry disproportionate weight in woodworking.
  • All three typefaces are SIL OFL 1.1: free to use, embed, and ship inside an application. Keep the license files with the fonts if you redistribute them.
